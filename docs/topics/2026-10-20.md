## 1. The Lake That Killed Overnight (Lake Nyos)

- **Type:** B
- **Hook (0-2 s):** "In 1986, a lake killed 1,800 people. No wave. No fire."
- **Surprising number:** nearly 1,800 people and 3,500 livestock killed by a gas cloud moving at more than 100 km/h (60 mph).
- **Metaphor (L10):** a soda bottle that has been shaken, then opened.
- **Outline:**
  1. Hook: a quiet crater lake at night, then the lake surface bulges and a pale cloud spills over the rim.
  2. How it works (1): a pocket of magma 80 km (50 miles) down leaks carbon dioxide, which seeps up with groundwater and collects at the lake bottom.
  3. How it works (2): the deep water holds more and more gas, like a sealed soda. On the evening of 21 Aug 1986 it burst out as one giant bubble.
  4. Big number: CO2 is heavier than air, so the cloud rolled downhill into the valley at 100+ km/h, killing nearly 1,800 people and 3,500 livestock.
  5. Impact: in 2001 scientists installed a pipe and fountain that lets the gas out slowly, and added two more pipes in 2011. About 10,000 people live there again because the soil is so fertile.
- **Impact facts:** nearly 1,800 dead, 3,500 livestock, "countless birds and insects". Degassing pipes went in in 2001 and 2011. About 10,000 people live in the area today.
- **Visual world:** a crater rim at night under stars with a side-view cut through the crater. The dithered layers of gas-heavy dark water must not look like the underwater diorama: keep it a solid geology cut, not a swim. A soda-bottle cutaway as the metaphor. A mist-white cloud flows downhill into a lit village and the lights go out one by one. It ends at dawn with a fountain jet on the lake. This is new: a crater cross-section plus a hillside village.
- **Note:** both this and Lituya Bay are "deadly water" stories. The mechanism and visuals are completely different, but don't schedule them back to back.
- **Sources:**
  - https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/images/86244/lake-nyos-cameroon (NASA Earth Observatory, public domain): verified for the date, nearly 1,800 people, 3,500 livestock, 100+ km/h, magma at 80 km, CO2 collecting at the bottom, pipes in 2001 and 2011, and 10,000 residents.

## 2. When the Sun Set Telegraphs on Fire (solar superstorms)

- **Type:** B
- **Hook:** "In 1859, the Sun set telegraph machines on fire."
- **Surprising number:** a Carrington-size storm today could cost nearly $2 trillion and leave 20-40 million Americans without power for 16 days to 1-2 years.
- **Metaphor:** a long wire acts like an antenna for a magnetic punch from the Sun. Show it as a garden hose (the solar wind) hitting an umbrella (Earth's magnetic field), with some spray getting through.
- **Outline:**
  1. Hook: a sparking telegraph key, then a flash of flame.
  2. How it works (1): the Sun throws out a CME, a cloud of charged particles that can weigh billions of tons and reach Earth in about three days.
  3. How it works (2): the cloud shakes Earth's magnetic field, which drives extra current into long wires. In September 1859 that shocked telegraph operators and set equipment on fire. Aurora were bright enough in the northeastern US to read a newspaper at night.
  4. Big number: on 13 March 1989 a smaller storm took down Quebec's grid in 90 seconds, leaving 6 million people without power for 9 hours.
  5. Impact: in July 2012 a storm at least as strong as 1859 missed Earth. Today one could cost about $2 trillion and black out 20-40 million Americans for weeks to years, which is why NOAA watches the Sun with satellites like DSCOVR.
- **Impact facts:** Quebec 1989: 6 million people, 9 hours, 90 seconds, transformers damaged in New Jersey. Today: about $2 trillion and 20-40 million people at risk (16 days to 1-2 years). The near miss of July 2012. In 2014 a scientist estimated the odds of a Carrington-class hit within 10 years at 12%. That is a 2014 estimate, so say it in the past tense.
- **Visual world:** a close-up of the Sun's surface (boiling granulation, a flare loop) as a new setting. Then a 19th-century telegraph office interior with sparks, lit by aurora through the window. Then a modern city skyline at night where the grid goes dark block by block under a green aurora.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/news/when-solar-storms-attack-space-weather-and-our-infrastructure (NOAA NESDIS, public domain): verified for 1859 telegraph fires, reading the newspaper by aurora, Quebec in 90 seconds with 6 million people for 9 hours, nearly $2 trillion, 20-40 million people for 16 days to 1-2 years, and the CME reaching Earth in three days.
  - https://www.weather.gov/safety/space (NWS, public domain): verified for September 1859 being the strongest storm on record, shocked technicians and equipment on fire, and 13 March 1989 with 6 million people for 9 hours and damaged transformers in NJ.
  - https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/planetary-science/23jul_superstorm/ (NASA, public domain): verified for the 2012 storm being "at least as strong as" 1859 but missing Earth, the 12% per decade figure (Riley 2014), and the $2 trillion figure being 20 times Hurricane Katrina.

## 3. Tonight's Shooting Stars Are Halley's Comet (Orionids)

- **Type:** C (the peak night is 21-22 Oct 2026, tomorrow night; the shower is active until 7 Nov)
- **Hook:** "The shooting stars this week are crumbs of Halley's Comet."
- **Surprising number:** they hit the air at 41 miles (66 km) per second. Separately, about 48.5 tons of space material falls on Earth every day.
- **Metaphor:** driving through falling snow at night. The flakes seem to come from one point in front of you, just as meteors seem to come from near Orion.
- **Outline:**
  1. Hook: a dark sky over a desert campsite, with a bright streak and a glowing trail.
  2. How it works (1): Halley's Comet leaves a trail of dust around its orbit, and every October Earth drives through it.
  3. How it works (2): grains as small as sand hit the air at 41 miles per second and burn up, and the fast ones leave glowing trains. Expect about 10-20 an hour under dark skies. Look near Betelgeuse in Orion before dawn.
  4. Big number: about 48.5 tons of space material lands on Earth every day.
  5. Impact: most of it is harmless dust, but in 2013 a house-sized rock exploded 14 miles above Chelyabinsk with the energy of about 440,000 tons of TNT. It blew out windows over 200 square miles and injured more than 1,600 people. That is why NASA tested asteroid deflection: in 2022 its DART spacecraft shortened an asteroid's orbit by 32 minutes. Halley itself returns in 2061.
- **Impact facts:** Chelyabinsk had more than 1,600 injured, mostly from broken glass, across 200 square miles, with about 440,000 t TNT equivalent. DART changed the orbit of Dimorphos from 11 h 55 min to 11 h 23 min, a difference of 32 min. Halley returns in 2061.
- **Visual world:** a desert night campsite with a parallax layer of Joshua trees, the mascot on a rock looking up, and Orion overhead. A cut out to a dusty comet ribbon in deep space: just the trail and the comet, no Earth globe. Then a snowy city morning with windows shattering.
- **Sources:**
  - https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/meteors-meteorites/orionids/ (NASA, public domain): verified for the Halley debris, active 2 Oct-7 Nov 2026 with the peak night 21-22 Oct, 10-20 per hour, 41 mi/s (66 km/s), glowing trains, and the radiant near Betelgeuse.
  - https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/meteors-meteorites/facts/ (NASA, public domain): verified for 48.5 tons per day and the Chelyabinsk numbers (11 mi/s, 14 miles up, 440,000 t TNT, 200 sq mi of windows, more than 1,600 injured).
  - https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/comets/1p-halley/ (NASA, public domain): verified for Halley last seen in 1986, returning in 2061, and its 76-year orbit.
  - https://science.nasa.gov/planetary-defense-dart/ (NASA, public domain): verified for the impact on 27 Sep 2022 and the orbit change from 11 h 55 to 11 h 23 (32 minutes).

## 4. Lightning Is Hotter Than the Sun

- **Type:** A
- **Hook:** "Lightning is five times hotter than the surface of the Sun."
- **Surprising number:** the air in the channel can reach 50,000 °F.
- **Metaphor:** a popping balloon. Air heated in a split second expands so fast it bangs, and that bang is thunder.
- **Outline:**
  1. Hook: a bolt hits a lone tree, with the thermometer slamming past the Sun icon.
  2. How it works (1): a flash carries about 100 million volts and heats the air around it to about 50,000 °F, five times hotter than the Sun's surface.
  3. How it works (2): the superheated air expands violently, then cools and contracts. That shock is thunder, which you can hear only about 10 miles away.
  4. Big number: lightning can strike 10 miles from its storm, and about 100 flashes hit the planet every minute.
  5. Impact: lightning starts about 10,000 forest fires a year. In the US it has killed an average of 27 people a year (2009-2018). About 90% of people struck survive, but many are left with lasting disability. "When thunder roars, go indoors."
- **Impact facts:** about 10,000 forest fires a year. US deaths averaged 27 a year (2009-2018) and 43 a year (1989-2018). Only about 10% of people struck are killed, and 90% live with some degree of disability. Lifetime odds of being struck are 1 in 15,300.
- **Visual world:** inside the thundercloud first (tumbling ice pellets and graupel, sparks of charge), then a prairie at night with a lone tree and farmhouse. The hot channel glows white, shock rings ripple out, then a forest edge catches fire. Keep it a ground-level night prairie, not a storm spiral.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.weather.gov/fwd/lightdidyouknow (NWS Fort Worth, public domain): verified for 50,000 °F "hotter than the surface of the sun", 100 million volts, 100 flashes per minute, 10 miles from the storm, 10,000 forest fires a year, and enough power to light a 100 W bulb for 3 months.
  - https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-science-thunder (NWS, public domain): verified that the discharge heats the air, which expands, and "5 times hotter than the surface of the sun", and that thunder is audible about 10 miles away.
  - https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-odds (NWS, public domain): verified for 27 deaths a year (2009-2018), 43 a year (1989-2018), 10% killed and 90% disabled, and lifetime odds of 1 in 15,300.

## 5. Why Death Valley Is So Hot

- **Type:** A
- **Hook:** "This ground once hit 201 degrees. Water boils at 212."
- **Surprising number:** 201 °F ground temperature (Furnace Creek, 15 July 1972) and 134 °F air, the world record (10 July 1913).
- **Metaphor:** an oven with the door shut. Hot air rises but the mountain walls keep it in, so it circulates and bakes again.
- **Outline:**
  1. Hook: a heat-shimmer salt flat with a ground thermometer racing to 201.
  2. How it works (1): the valley is a long, narrow basin 282 feet below sea level, walled in by high, steep mountains.
  3. How it works (2): the clear, dry air and few plants let the sun heat the ground, and the rocks radiate that heat back. Hot air rises, gets trapped by the walls and circulates back down, getting hotter each time.
  4. Big number: 134 °F air, the hottest ever recorded on Earth. At midnight it is often still 100-110 °F.
  5. Impact: on average someone dies of suspected heat illness here about every other year. Above 120 °F outdoor work stops, and in extreme heat rescue helicopters can't get enough lift to fly.
- **Impact facts:** a heat death about every other year. Rangers handle overheated visitors several times a week in summer. Helicopters sometimes can't fly. Non-emergency outdoor labour is banned above 120 °F. The recent highs were 130 °F (16 Aug 2020 and 9 Jul 2021).
- **Visual world:** a desert basin side view, new for us: layered parallax mountains (far range hazy and purple, near rocks dark red), a cracked salt flat, visible heat-shimmer currents looping inside the valley like an oven, and a sun disc that glows harder. The mascot sweats on a rock.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.nps.gov/deva/learn/nature/weather-and-climate.htm (NPS, public domain): verified for 201 °F ground (15 Jul 1972), 134 °F (10 Jul 1913), 282 ft below sea level, the "Why so hot?" mechanism, and less than 2 in of rain a year.
  - https://www.nps.gov/deva/learn/news/heat-press-kit.htm (NPS, public domain): verified that topography is the main cause, a death about every other year, the helicopter limits, the 120 °F work rule, nights of 100-110 °F, and the 130 °F readings in 2020 and 2021.

## 6. The Blast With No Crater (Tunguska)

- **Type:** B (overlaps with #3; pick one)
- **Hook:** "In 1908, something flattened 830 square miles of forest, and left no crater."
- **Surprising number:** 830 square miles of trees knocked down by a rock only about 120 feet (37 m) across.
- **Metaphor:** a firecracker going off above a field of matchsticks. They all fall flat, pointing away from the blast.
- **Outline:**
  1. Hook: a Siberian forest at dawn, a flash, and a wall of trees falling.
  2. How it works (1): at about 7:15 am on 30 June 1908, a space rock about 120 ft wide and 220 million pounds entered the air over Siberia.
  3. How it works (2): it never hit the ground. It exploded a few miles up, so the shock wave pushed the trees flat and outward, but there was no crater.
  4. Big number: a butterfly-shaped zone of 830 square miles, with millions of trees down 9-22 miles from the centre.
  5. Impact: hundreds of reindeer died, and the remote location probably spared people. Over a city, damage like this would be catastrophic. That is why NASA tracks space rocks and, in 2022, proved it can nudge one (DART, 32 minutes).
- **Impact facts:** 830 sq mi, millions of trees, hundreds of reindeer killed, no confirmed human deaths. The DART deflection was 32 minutes.
- **Visual world:** a taiga forest at dawn (new): a parallax pine layer, fog, and a reindeer herd. A top-down shot of the radial "butterfly" of fallen trees, then an imagined city overlay at the same scale, with the scale shown by a ghosted skyline, not a map.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.nasa.gov/history/115-years-ago-the-tunguska-asteroid-impact-event/ (NASA History, public domain text; images on the page are third-party, don't use them): verified for 7:15 am on 30 Jun 1908, the 830 sq mi butterfly-shaped area 9-22 miles from the epicentre, millions of trees, and a mid-air explosion.
  - https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/meteors-meteorites/facts/ (NASA, public domain): verified that it exploded a few miles up, was about 120 ft (37 m) and 220 million lb, and killed hundreds of reindeer with no evidence of human deaths.
  - https://science.nasa.gov/planetary-defense-dart/ (NASA, public domain): verified for the 32-minute deflection.

## 7. The Mountain That Fell Sideways (Mount St. Helens)

- **Type:** B
- **Hook:** "In 1980, this mountain lost 1,314 feet off its top in seconds."
- **Surprising number:** the largest landslide on Earth in recorded history, followed by a sideways blast of at least 300 mph.
- **Metaphor:** a pressure cooker with the lid knocked off sideways. When the side gave way, the pressure blasted out the side, not the top.
- **Outline:**
  1. Hook: a snowy cone, then the whole north face starts to slide.
  2. How it works (1): magma rose inside the mountain. More than 10,000 earthquakes followed and the north flank bulged out at least 450 feet.
  3. How it works (2): at 8:32 am on 18 May 1980, a magnitude 5.1 quake shook the bulge loose. It slid away as the largest landslide in recorded history, uncorking the magma.
  4. Big number: the lateral blast reached at least 300 mph, covered 230 square miles and reached 17 miles out. The summit dropped from 9,677 to 8,363 feet.
  5. Impact: 57 people died, along with about 7,000 big-game animals and 12 million hatchery salmon. More than 4 billion board feet of timber was damaged, and the damage came to about $1.1 billion.
- **Impact facts:** 57 dead, about 7,000 big game, about 12 million salmon fingerlings, 4 billion board feet of timber, and a refined estimate of $1.1 billion. The landslide buried 14 miles of the North Fork Toutle valley to an average of 150 ft.
- **Visual world:** a volcano cross-section (new): magma chamber, glowing dike, the bulge growing frame by frame. Then the side collapse and a blast wave flattening a parallax forest, and an ash column at the end.
- **Sources:**
  - https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/2000/fs036-00/ (USGS Fact Sheet 036-00, public domain): verified for the largest landslide in recorded history, the M5.1 quake at 8:32 am, more than 10,000 quakes, the 450 ft bulge, at least 300 mph, 230 sq mi and 17 miles, summit 9,677 to 8,363 ft (1,314 ft removed), 57 human deaths, about 7,000 big game, and about 12 million salmon fingerlings.
  - https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/msh/impact.html (USGS, public domain): verified for 57 lives, more than 4 billion board feet of timber, the $1.1 billion refined cost, and nearly 7,000 big-game animals.

## 8. Bats: The $3.7 Billion Night Shift

- **Type:** B (Bat Week is the last week of October, but that timing note is not a script fact)
- **Hook:** "One bat can eat its own weight in bugs in a single night."
- **Surprising number:** bats save US farmers at least $3.7 billion a year in pest control.
- **Metaphor:** a living bug vacuum. Show a swarm over a cornfield being "vacuumed" by one bat.
- **Outline:**
  1. Hook: a bat swoops through a cloud of moths at dusk.
  2. How it works (1): bats hunt insects at night and can eat up to their own body weight in insects each night.
  3. How it works (2): that pest control protects crops and forests, and is worth at least $3.7 billion a year to US farmers.
  4. Big number: a fungal disease, white-nose syndrome, killed an estimated 5.7-6.7 million North American bats by 2012. Sick bats wake from hibernation and fly out in winter daylight.
  5. Impact: with bats gone, millions of insects that would have been eaten feed on trees and crops instead, which hits forestry, farming and even human health. Some species are pushed to the brink of extinction.
- **Impact facts:** at least $3.7 billion a year in pest control. 5.7-6.7 million bats dead (2012 estimate), with "millions" in the 2019 wording. Insects then feed on crops and trees. Some species are near extinction.
- **Visual world:** a cave interior (new): stalactites, a hanging cluster of bats breathing slowly, and a dusk emergence out of the cave mouth over moonlit farmland. A microscope zoom on the white fungus on a bat's nose.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.fws.gov/story/2019-10/service-announces-100000-challenge-save-nations-bats (US Fish and Wildlife Service, public domain): verified for at least $3.7 billion a year, "a bat can possibly eat its own body weight in insects each night", WNS killing millions, species near extinction, and insects feeding on trees and crops.
  - https://www.fws.gov/story/2012-01/north-american-bat-death-toll-exceeds-55-million-white-nose-syndrome (USFWS, public domain): verified for 5.7-6.7 million bats dead, and sick bats flying in winter daylight and clustering at cave entrances.
- **Note:** the hook says "can eat". FWS says "can possibly", so keep the hedge: "up to its own weight".

## 9. Superbugs: Germs That Beat Our Medicine

- **Type:** A
- **Hook:** "Every year, 35,000 Americans die from germs our medicine can't kill."
- **Surprising number:** more than 2.8 million drug-resistant infections a year in the US. Worldwide, at least 1.27 million deaths in 2019.
- **Metaphor:** a video game where a few players find a cheat code, survive, and pass the code to others.
- **Outline:**
  1. Hook: a microscope view with a wave of antibiotic sweeping through. Most bacteria pop, but one glows and survives.
  2. How it works (1): antibiotics kill most germs, but any that carry resistance traits in their DNA survive and multiply.
  3. How it works (2): those survivors can pass the resistance genes to other germs, so resistance spreads. Every use of antibiotics adds to this pressure.
  4. Big number: in the US, more than 2.8 million resistant infections and more than 35,000 deaths a year. With C. diff included, more than 3 million infections and 48,000 deaths.
  5. Impact: worldwide, at least 1.27 million deaths in 2019. In the US, treating just six resistant germs costs more than $4.6 billion a year. One drug-resistant fungus rose nearly five-fold from 2019 to 2022.
- **Impact facts:** as listed above, all from the CDC facts page.
- **Visual world:** a microscope or petri-dish world (new): pixel bacteria as glowing blobs, an antibiotic tide, and a plasmid ring passed between cells like a glowing key. Zoom out to a hospital window at night.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.cdc.gov/antimicrobial-resistance/data-research/facts-stats/index.html (CDC, public domain): verified for more than 2.8 million infections, more than 35,000 deaths, more than 3 million and 48,000 with C. diff, 1.27 million worldwide (CDC citing The Lancet), more than $4.6 billion, and the nearly five-fold rise of the drug-resistant fungus from 2019 to 2022.
  - https://www.cdc.gov/antimicrobial-resistance/about/index.html (CDC, public domain): verified for the mechanism (survivors multiply, and resistance traits in DNA spread to other germs).
- **Note:** the 2.8 million and 35,000 figures are CDC's 2019 report estimates. Say "about 35,000 a year (CDC estimate)".

## 10. Why the Ground Swallows Houses (sinkholes)

- **Type:** A
- **Hook:** "Sinkholes cost the US at least 300 million dollars a year."
- **Surprising number:** at least $300 million a year in damage (the average over 15 years), and probably much higher.
- **Metaphor:** a sugar cube under a rug. Water slowly dissolves it until the rug drops.
- **Outline:**
  1. Hook: a suburban street at night, and a house sags into the ground.
  2. How it works (1): in "karst" areas the bedrock is limestone or similar rock that slightly acidic rainwater slowly dissolves.
  3. How it works (2): hidden caves grow under the soil until the roof can't hold it, then it collapses.
  4. Big number: at least $300 million a year in US damage. The real total is probably higher because nobody tracks it nationally.
  5. Impact: damage is concentrated in a few states (Florida, Texas, Alabama, Missouri, Kentucky, Tennessee and Pennsylvania), and sinkhole risk depends on where you live.
- **Visual world:** an underground cross-section (new): layered soil over limestone, rain seeping down, a cave cavity growing, and a street above with houses and car lights. A sugar-cube cut-in as the metaphor.
- **Sources:**
  - https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70155910 (USGS Publications Warehouse, Weary 2015, public domain abstract): verified for "at least $300,000,000 per year" over the last 15 years, "actual total is probably much higher", and the comparison with other hazards.
