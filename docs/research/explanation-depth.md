# Explanation depth: what to explain, and how much, in a 50-60 s reel

Research summary, October 2026. Companion to docs/research/retention-research.md (same grades).
**[PR]** peer-reviewed. **[IND]** platform/industry. **[EXP]** expert opinion or our inference.
**[MYTH]** folklore. "Abstract-level" means only the abstract or a summary was read.

**Bottom line.** Explaining the mechanism (the causal chain) beats describing the outcome, both for
understanding and for how good the explanation feels. One well-built mechanism fits in under a minute.
The limits come from working memory. Use 3-4 causal links, at most 2 new technical terms, one
analogy that is explicitly mapped onto the real thing, and no detail that does not carry a cause.
Sounding technical is not the same as explaining. Viewers reward both, so the risk is on us.

## 1. Mechanism vs description

## 2. How much detail and jargon

## 3. Analogies and metaphors

- **Structure mapping.** An analogy carries over *relations* ("pressure holds gas in"), not looks
  (Gentner 1983, *Cognitive Science* 7:155-170, https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/Gentner83.2b.pdf). [PR]
- **Map it explicitly, and say where it breaks.** State the correspondences ("the X is the Y") and
  name the wrong inferences it invites (boiling water as mantle convection suggests a liquid mantle).
  Source: Jee et al. 2010, *J. Geoscience Education* 58:2-13,
  https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/uttal/documents/Jeeetal2010.pdf. [PR review]
- **An analogy alone does not teach facts.** Climate analogies did not raise factual knowledge and had
  weak effects (Raimi, Stern & Maki 2017, *PLoS ONE* 12:e0171130,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5279784/). [PR] The real mechanism must follow within a line.

## 4. Numbers and technical terms

- **Perspective sentences** ("about the height of...") improve recall and estimation of numbers.
  Source: Barrio et al. 2016 (retention-research.md §5). [PR]
- **Precise numbers read as confident.** People judged precise answers as more confident and
  preferred their advice. Source: Jerez-Fernandez, Angulo & Oppenheimer 2014, *Psychological Science*
  25:633-635; summary: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/were-only-human/precisely-right-no-doubt-trust-me.html.
  [PR, abstract-level] Risk: false precision. Keep sourced numbers exactly as the source gives them.
- **Magnitudes, not maths.** Formulas hurt (Mayer & Jackson); a number must be a chain step or the
  peak. [EXP]
- **Term plus gloss.** Definitions did not restore fluency (Bullock 2019), so a definition does not
  license more jargon. Pre-training supports introducing a term *before* the process uses it. Best
  form: plain words first, then the term ("rising air, an updraft"), then reuse the term once as a
  highlighted caption (signalling). The exact form is untested. [EXP, inferred from PR]

## 5. Structure for a short explainer

- **Question → mechanism → consequence.** Curiosity needs a half-known gap (retention-research.md §3),
  and the illusion of explanatory depth makes "why" that gap. [PR + EXP]
- **Chain length.** Ranney's 35 words use 3 links. Zemla shows viewers want enough causes to make the
  outcome feel inevitable. Cowan caps it at about 4. **Use 3-4 links.** [EXP, inferred]
- **Segment.** One link per "level" (d=0.79 for segmenting). [PR, adapted]
- **Restate the chain.** Close by answering the hook with the whole chain in one sentence, the way a
  worked example ends on its result. [EXP]
- Connectives ("so", "because") are signposts. The evidence on them is mixed (Degand & Sanders 2002). [PR]
- **Reach.** On one TikTok account, ~60 s lecture-style geoscience videos did best on reach and
  engagement. Source: Zawacki et al. 2022, *Geoscience Communication* 5:363-380,
  https://gc.copernicus.org/articles/5/363/2022/. [PR]

## 6. Pauses and the call to action

- Words heard after a silent pause were recognised better later (MacGregor, Corley & Donaldson 2010,
  *Neuropsychologia* 48:3982-3992, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20950633/). [PR]
- Fillers ("uh") improved recall; coughs of equal length hurt it, so the gain is attention, not time.
  Keep pauses clean, never an SFX hit (Fraundorf & Watson 2011, *JML* 65:161-175,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3134332/). [PR]
- Pauses and visual cues both drive the segmenting benefit (Spanjers et al. 2012, *Computers &
  Education* 59:274-280, https://repub.eur.nl/pub/70813) [PR, abstract-level]. 3 s pauses about every
  23 words aided second-language listening (Blau 1990, *TESOL Quarterly* 24:746-753,
  https://doi.org/10.2307/3587129) [PR, via secondary].
- **CTA timing.** No peer-reviewed evidence. TikTok advises a hook first and a clear CTA at the end,
  https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices. [IND] A ~1 s pause before the follow
  line keeps the takeaway as the "end" moment (peak-end rule). [EXP]

## 7. Depth, trust and the seductive-allure risk

- Irrelevant neuroscience made bad explanations more satisfying to non-experts. Source: Weisberg et
  al. 2008, *J. Cognitive Neuroscience* 20:470-477, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2778755/.
  The effect holds across sciences for any **reductive** detail (smaller parts, more basic processes).
  Source: Hopkins, Weisberg & Taylor 2016, *Cognition* 155:67-76, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27367591/. [PR]
- Explanatory satisfaction tracks *perceived* learning, with only tenuous links to *actual* learning.
  Perceived learning fully explains the preference for reductive detail. Source: Liquin & Lombrozo
  2022, *Cognitive Psychology* 132:101453, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34875484/. [PR]
- Perceived "scientificness" feeds credibility judgements (Thomm & Bromme 2012, *Science Education*
  96:187-211, https://doi.org/10.1002/sce.20480) [PR, abstract-level]. Easy science depictions make
  lay readers over-trust their own judgement (Scharrer et al. 2012, *Learning and Instruction*
  22:231-243, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2011.11.004) [PR, via secondary].
- **Net.** Real mechanism raises perceived quality (Zemla) and understanding (Ranney). Technical words
  without causal work raise satisfaction, not learning. Every technical word must do causal work, and
  one honest "nobody knows exactly" protects trust. [EXP, inferred]

## (a) Script depth recipe (8 scenes, 50-60 s)

| Beat | Job | Words |
|---|---|---|
| hook | Stake number plus a **why/how** question the mechanism answers | 18-22 |
| how 1 (ingredients) | Name the 1-2 parts, plain words first then the term (pre-training) | 15-22 |
| payoff (metaphor) | One everyday object, explicitly mapped ("the X is the Y"), plus causal link 1 | 20-27 |
| how 2 (tipping point) | Causal links 2-3: what changes, and why it is sudden. Admit unknowns. | 20-25 |
| peak | Surprising number plus perspective, then a 0.6 s pause | 10-16 |
| answer | Close the hook: the whole chain in one sentence | 14-18 |
| aftermath | Consequence, and *why* it lasts or *why* the fix works | 20-28 |
| outro | Takeaway, then a 1.0 s pause, then "Follow Eight Bit Explains." | 9-11 |

**Totals.** 140-155 words, 3-4 causal links, at most 2 technical terms. **Timing (measured from the
ep005-007 manifests).** 175-180 wpm voiced plus about 9 s of gaps, so 150 words is about 60 s. More
needs `speed` 1.2 or shorter scene tails. L15 says 45-55 s: depth versus length is the owner's call.

**Pauses.** 0.2 s between lines (current). About 0.75 s at scene changes (current lead plus tail).
**Add** 0.5-0.6 s after the "click" line of the mechanism and after the peak line. **Add** 1.0 s before
the follow line. **Pipeline bug:** tools/tts.py only inserts the 1.0 s outro pause when the outro has 2
or more `vo` lines. All three episodes put the takeaway and the follow line in one string, so the
pause never fires. Split them into two lines. Per-line pauses would need a new optional field; this
is not implemented.

### Checklist (checkable on the script)

1. The hook asks *how* or *why*, and the answer beat answers that exact question.
2. The mechanism has 3-4 causal links, and each link sits in its own sentence with "so", "because"
   or "which".
3. Every outcome verb (burned, exploded, blacked out, killed) has its cause in the same or the next
   sentence.
4. At most 2 technical terms. Each one is glossed in plain words the first time, appears before the
   step that uses it, and is reused once as the highlighted caption word.
5. One metaphor. It is mapped explicitly and followed by the real mechanism within the next line. If
   it invites a wrong inference, say where it breaks.
6. No formulas, and no reductive word that does no causal work (delete-test: if removing it loses
   nothing, remove it).
7. Every number matches `sources` verbatim, no sentence carries more than two numbers, and each
   number is either a causal step, a consequence, or the peak with a perspective.
8. Any unknown is stated once ("nobody knows exactly...").
9. The answer beat restates the full chain in one sentence.
10. 140-155 words, voiced rate 160-185 wpm, total 60 s or less.
11. The outro `vo` has two lines (takeaway, follow), so the 1.0 s pause fires. The follow line lasts
    1 s or less.
12. Every fact added for depth cites a public-domain US government URL in `sources`.

## (b) Critique and rewrites

Every number is kept exactly as worded now. " / " separates lines; scenes and beats are unchanged.

### ep005 The lake that killed overnight (now 133 words)

**Said WHAT, not WHY**
- "piles up at the bottom": why the gas doesn't just bubble away (water pressure keeps it dissolved,
  and the deep layer doesn't mix).
- "like a sealed soda": the metaphor is never mapped. What is the cap?
- "didn't fizz. It exploded" / "burst out one giant bubble": the key why is missing. Deep water rose,
  its pressure dropped, and the gas came out of solution all at once.
- "pipes let the gas out slowly": why that keeps people safe (the gas can't build up again).

**Rewrite (152 words)**
1. In nineteen eighty-six, a quiet lake killed almost eighteen hundred people in one evening. / No wave. No fire. So how?
2. Fifty miles down, magma leaks carbon dioxide. / It seeps up into the lake's deepest water.
3. Down there, the water's weight squeezes the gas in, like a soda cap. / And the deep layer doesn't mix with the top, so the gas keeps building.
4. On August twenty-first, that deep water rose. The pressure dropped, and the gas burst out at once. / Nobody knows exactly what tipped it.
5. Carbon dioxide is heavier than air, so it poured downhill, / at over sixty miles an hour.
6. It pushed away the air people breathed. / Nearly eighteen hundred people suffocated, and thirty-five hundred animals.
7. Since two thousand one, pipes let the gas out slowly, so it can't build up again. / Today about ten thousand people live there again.
8. The quietest lake can be the deadliest. / Follow Eight Bit Explains.

**New facts.** Overturn depressurises deep water and frees the gas, and mixing prevents build-up
("doesn't mix" is inferred from that): USGS OFR 97-487 p. 16,
https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1997/of97-487/data/OFR97-487.pdf. The eruption may need no external
trigger, and degassing reduced the CO2: USGS, https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70032981.
Suffocation and steady-rate pipes: NASA, already in `sources`.

### ep006 When the Sun set telegraphs on fire (now 135 words)

**Said WHAT, not WHY**
- "A big hit shakes it, pushing extra current into long wires": *how* a shaking field makes current
  is the core why, and it is missing. A changing magnetic field induces current in a wire, the same
  thing a generator does. Also missing: why some storms hit harder (a south-pointing field).
- "telegraphs caught fire": we don't say that the current ran through the telegraph wires.
- "blacked out Quebec in ninety seconds": why a grid fails (transformers pushed out of their
  designed range).
- "for weeks or longer": why so long (giant transformers can take years to repair).

**Rewrite (159 words; with the current gaps about 63 s, so needs `speed` 1.2 or shorter tails)**
1. In eighteen fifty-nine, the Sun set telegraph machines on fire. / Could it happen again, to our power grid?
2. The Sun can hurl billions of tons of charged particles, with their own magnetic field. / They arrive in about three days.
3. When that field points south, it hits Earth's magnetic field hardest, / and our field shakes.
4. A shaking magnetic field pushes current through long wires, the same trick a generator uses. / In eighteen fifty-nine, that current shocked operators and lit telegraphs.
5. In nineteen eighty-nine, a smaller storm pushed power transformers out of their safe range. / Quebec went dark in ninety seconds. Six million people lost power for nine hours.
6. And in twenty twelve, a storm that strong missed us by about a week.
7. A direct hit today could cost two trillion dollars, and black out up to forty million Americans for weeks or longer. / Giant transformers can take years to repair.
8. So satellites now watch the Sun. / Follow Eight Bit Explains.

**New facts.** Embedded field, south-pointing is strongest: https://www.spaceweather.gov/phenomena/coronal-mass-ejections.
Fluctuating fields induce current in long wires, 1859 shocks: https://www.weather.gov/safety/space.
Generators induce current the same way: https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/how-electricity-is-generated.php.
Transformers pushed out of their design range: https://www.spaceweather.gov/impacts/electric-power-transmission.
Repairs take years: NASA, already in `sources`.

### ep007 Lightning is hotter than the Sun (now 134 words)

**Said WHAT, not WHY**
- **The hook asks "how does it get that hot?" and the script never answers it.** "A hundred million
  volts" and "fifty thousand degrees" are two numbers with no link between them. The missing link:
  the flash's energy heats the air in its channel in a few millionths of a second.
- "Light ice turns positive and rises": why it rises (updrafts lift the light ice; the heavy hail
  stays low).
- "When the charge lets go": why it lets go (air is an insulator until the charge difference gets too
  big).
- The thunder comes before the heat that causes it. The rewrite puts it after the heat.

**Rewrite (155 words)**
1. Lightning is five times hotter than the surface of the Sun. / So how does it get that hot, and why the boom?
2. Inside a storm cloud, updrafts of rising air smash ice and small hail together. / Light ice turns positive and rides up. Heavy hail turns negative and stays low.
3. Air is an insulator: it holds the charges apart until the difference gets too big. / Then it fails all at once: one giant spark.
4. One flash carries about a hundred million volts. / Its energy heats the air in its path in a few millionths of a second,
5. to fifty thousand degrees Fahrenheit. / Five times the Sun's surface.
6. Air heated that fast explodes outward, like a popping balloon. / That bang is thunder.
7. That heat starts about ten thousand forest fires a year. / And lightning killed about twenty-seven Americans a year from two thousand nine to twenty eighteen.
8. When thunder roars, go indoors. / Follow Eight Bit Explains.

The line "most people struck survive" (no number) was cut for length. Add it back if `speed` goes up.

**New facts.** Collisions, updrafts lifting the smaller ice, heating "in only a few millionths of a
second", air exploding outward: NOAA NSSL, https://www.nssl.noaa.gov/education/svrwx101/lightning/
and https://www.nssl.noaa.gov/education/svrwx101/lightning/faq/. Air insulates until the charge
difference is too large: NWS, https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-science-overview.
