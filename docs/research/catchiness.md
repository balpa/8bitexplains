# Catchiness: what gets watched, sent, commented and followed

Research summary, 26 October 2026. Asked by the owner after ep005-ep007: "they were generally
nice, but we need additions to make them catchy". Retention mechanics (hook, pacing, open loops,
peak-end) are in `retention-research.md` and distribution in `growth-and-data.md`; this file
covers the gap: which topics and framings pull views, and what to build into a video so viewers
send it, comment and follow.

**Grades** as in growth-and-data.md: [OFF] platform statement, [PR] peer-reviewed, [IND] industry
data or trade press, [EXP] practitioner opinion or our inference, [MYTH] untraceable.

## What the platforms reward (recap, one line each)

- Instagram: watch time, likes per reach and **sends per reach** are the three signals Mosseri
  named (Jan 2025, reported by Social Media Today [S1, IND]). Sends weigh slightly more for
  non-followers, which is all of our audience at the start. Meta's own list also predicts
  comments, reshares, follows and "Use audio" taps (growth-and-data.md S6 [OFF]).
- TikTok: completion, shares, comments, skips; follows drive personalisation (growth-and-data.md).
- "Sends are worth 3-5x likes" is still [MYTH]: no coefficient has been published [S1].

So the extra levers beyond retention are **sends** (most of all), **comments** and **follows**.

## 1. Framing: what the title promises

- **Self-relevance raises sharing, causally.** Six studies (3,727 US adults, 362 messages): how
  relevant a message feels to me and to people I know each predicts intention to share; a
  preregistered framing manipulation that raised relevance raised sharing intentions [S2, PR,
  Cosme, Scholz, Falk et al. 2023, *J Exp Psych: General* 152(1):253-267]. A 2025 follow-up found
  the same framing moves actual sharing, across cultures and topics [S3, PR, *PNAS Nexus*].
- **The biggest faceless animated channels all frame in second person.** Zack D. Films (3D
  shorts, ~29M subscribers, ~19.5M average views per short): "what would happen if" and "why you
  can't ..." titles. NYKentertain (created Apr 2025, 1.5B views in ~18 months): "Can you survive
  a tornado inside a shipping container?". Zenn: "What the world's quietest room does to your
  brain". The Paint Explainer: "... places on Earth you can't visit" [S4, S5, IND snapshots,
  figures disagree slightly between sources].
- **Our hooks today are third-person history**: "In 1986, a lake killed 1,800 people." Strong
  "wait, what?", but the viewer is a spectator. The same science reframed puts them inside it:
  "What happens to your body if lightning hits you?", "Could your phone survive the 1859 solar
  storm?", "You'd have 2 minutes to escape this lake." [EXP, from S2-S5]
- **Topic families that recur among top performers**: your body and brain, survival scenarios,
  space and scale, everyday "why" [S4, S5, S6, IND/anecdotal; no cross-channel dataset exists].
  Our disaster stories fit "survival" when the viewer is placed in them.

## 2. Emotion: what makes people send it

- **High-arousal emotions are shared; low-arousal ones are not.** Awe (positive) and anxiety or
  anger (negative) raise sharing; sadness lowers it. It held after controlling for usefulness,
  surprise and interest, which also help [S7, PR, Berger & Milkman 2012, *JMR*, ~7,000 NYT
  articles plus experiments].
- **Implication for us** [EXP]: the peak should feel like awe or "that could be me" tension
  (a scale reveal, a countdown), not grief. A death toll on its own reads sad; pair it with the
  scale image or the survival angle. The aftermath can end on awe or relief (ep005's degassing
  fountain is a good model).
- **Practical utility** is shared too [S7]. A survival frame gives a usable takeaway ("crouch, feet
  together", "get uphill") that justifies sending it to someone.

## 3. Interaction inside the video

- **Prediction prompts work.** Students asked to guess before each 2-minute video segment, almost
  always wrongly and with no feedback, remembered more of the whole video, not only the asked
  facts [S8, PR, Carpenter & Toftness 2017, *JARMAC*]. A meta-analysis puts the effect on the
  asked content at g ≈ 0.58; benefits on other content are smaller in long lectures [S9, PR
  summaries]. A 50 s reel is closer to the short segments.
- **A guess is also the cheapest comment**: "How hot is it? Guess before the bird does." gives a
  one-word reply anyone can type [EXP]. Evidence that in-video CTAs raise comments is
  practitioner-only [S10, IND]; no peer-reviewed test was found.
- **Placement** [EXP]: the guess goes right before the peak number (30-42 s), the answer lands on
  the peak. The question prompt sits in the caption band as the one extra text block (L19).
  The ending stays clean (peak-end rule, retention-research.md §7): no "comment below" plea after
  the takeaway. The comment ask goes in the post caption and a pinned comment instead.

## 4. Follows: give a reason to come back

- **Named, recurring formats** [EXP, S4]: top channels repeat a title template ("Can you
  survive ...", "Things we all did as kids") so the next one is recognisable. A series name on
  the cover and in the HUD ("COULD YOU SURVIVE? #3") tells the viewer there are more.
- **Multi-part videos lose most viewers between parts** (one creator: 80K then 21K) [S11, IND,
  n = 1]. Keep every episode standalone; link episodes by series name, not by "part 2".
- **Mascot personality** [EXP]: the bird is our only recurring character. A small comic beat per
  episode (it panics, gets zapped, faints) gives a reason to like the channel and not only the
  video. TikTok engagement leans on humour [S12, IND].

## 5. Humour (added after the owner asked for it)

- **Related humour helps, unrelated humour does not.** Lectures with humour tied to the content
  beat neutral versions on motivation and recall (25 undergraduates, within-subjects, EEG) [S13,
  PR, small]. Four jokes unrelated to the content of an instructional video changed neither
  learning nor motivation [S14, dissertation]. Reviews call the overall evidence mixed; the safe
  reading is "joke about the thing you explain" [EXP].
- **Our form of it** [EXP]: physical comedy on the mascot, which is wordless and costs no
  narration time (the 60 s limit is already tight), plus at most a dry line that replaces words.

## 6. What not to do

- No bait that the video does not pay off: Instagram predicts "Not interested" taps and its
  guidelines down-rank misleading framing (growth-and-data.md §1). Every "you" hook must be
  answered in the video.
- No trending music: we only use our own chiptune (licence rule). Original audio also earns the
  "Use audio" signal if someone reuses it.
- Do not chase comments with controversy; anger shares, but it does not fit the brand and invites
  "Not interested".

## Proposed changes (for the owner to choose)

1. **"You" framing** for hook, title and cover: every episode puts the viewer inside the event.
   Same science, same sourcing rules.
2. **Guess moment** before the peak number: an on-screen question, a 1.5 s countdown, then the
   reveal. Post caption and pinned comment ask for the guess.
3. **Awe peak, not grief peak**: the peak is a scale reveal or a survival countdown; death tolls
   move to the aftermath with the "what changed" line.
4. **One survival or "what you should do" takeaway** where the topic allows it (shareable utility).
5. **Series name**: start one named format (working title "COULD YOU SURVIVE?") on the cover and
   HUD; non-survival topics keep the plain format.
6. **Bird comedy beat**: one gag per episode tied to the story.
7. **Measure it**: once 200 followers unlock Trial Reels, post the same episode with a third-person
   and a "you" hook to non-followers and compare sends per reach (growth-and-data.md §5).
